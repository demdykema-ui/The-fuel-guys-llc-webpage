What Happens If a Backup Generator Runs Out of Fuel Mid-Outage?

Quick answer

The engine stops and the building goes dark until utility power comes back. Running a tank to the bottom pulls air into the injection system, so the generator will not restart the moment fuel arrives — the fuel system has to be bled and primed first. Plan on hours, not minutes.

By Erick Dykema Published Aug 12, 2026 Updated Aug 12, 2026 6 min read Reliability & Operations

A generator that runs dry in a parking lot is an inconvenience. One that runs dry at 2 a.m. during a Front Range outage, while it is carrying a building's emergency loads, is a different animal. The lights go out a second time. The elevator stops wherever it is. And the machine that was supposed to fix the problem has become the problem — because a diesel engine that has pumped its tank to the bottom does not simply start back up when fuel shows up. That last part is what catches people, and it is worth understanding before you need it.

What happens in the first minute?

Fuel pressure falls off, the engine stumbles, and it shuts down. The transfer switch sees the emergency source fail and drops the load. Utility power is still out, so there is nothing to transfer back to. The building sits dark on whatever battery-backed lighting and UPS runtime it has, which is measured in minutes.

The panel usually logs this honestly — low fuel, then a shutdown. Cummins' operator manual for its DQF-series standby sets spells out what those warnings are supposed to buy you. A LOW FUEL alarm trips at roughly 62 percent of tank level. A CRITICAL LOW FUEL alarm means the level "has dropped to tank bottom," and the manual states the point of it plainly: to give "the operator time to shut down generator set before fuel runs out, preventing loss of prime or engine damage."

Panel indicationWhat it meansWhat it is telling you to do
LOW FUELLevel has dropped below the low-fuel setpoint (about 62% on the Cummins DQF sub-base tank)Order fuel now, while the engine is still running normally
CRITICAL LOW FUELLevel has dropped to tank bottomShut down on your terms before the engine takes air, or get fuel there within minutes
Low fuel shutdown / engine stoppedThe engine has already lost its supplyFuel plus a bleed-and-prime, and likely filters

Read that middle row again. The critical alarm is not a countdown timer. It is the last point at which shutting the set down deliberately is cheaper than letting it run itself out.

Why won't it restart as soon as you add fuel?

Because it is full of air. Cummins' manual describes exactly this failure: continued operation at a critical low fuel level allows "air to enter the engine injection pump necessitating bleeding to restart the engine." Utah State University Extension's fact sheet on bleeding diesel fuel systems puts the mechanics plainly — when an engine runs out of fuel, air is trapped in the lines, and "when you try to start the engine this air acts as a lock, preventing the normal supply of fuel into the cylinder." Cranking harder does not clear it. It runs the batteries down and heats the starter.

The fix is a procedure, not a step. The general shape: shut the fuel valve, replace the filter elements, open the bleed plug on the filter nearest the tank, work the hand primer until fuel runs clean with no bubbles, then move outward through the system. If it still will not fire, crack the injection lines at the injectors, crank until fuel appears, and tighten them back down one at a time. The Extension sheet carries a caution worth repeating: "No attempt should be made to service the injection pump or nozzles. This requires special tools and know how."

Every engine is different — some newer electronic sets have priming pumps that do most of this for you, some older mechanical ones need the full manual sequence. The only correct procedure is the one in your engine's operator manual, and the time to find that manual is not during an outage. Print the fuel-system page and tape it inside the enclosure door.

What does the bottom of the tank do to your filters?

The last few inches of a storage tank are the worst fuel in the tank. Water settles out on the bottom, and sediment and microbial growth collect with it. NFPA 110 addresses this directly: Section 7.9.1.2 requires that "fuel system design shall provide for a supply of clean fuel to the prime mover," and Section 8.3.7 requires a fuel quality test at least annually using appropriate ASTM standards.

The practical consequence: an engine that ran itself to the bottom of the tank pulled that layer through its filters on the way down. So the restart is often not just a bleed — it is a bleed plus a fuel filter and water separator change, with a real chance the new filter plugs again once the tank is refilled and the sediment is stirred up. Add that to your time estimate.

What is the transfer switch doing while all this is going on?

Not what most people assume. The building does not light up the instant the lineman closes the recloser. The transfer switch runs a set of time delays first, and on a typical ASCO 7000-series controller the factory defaults look like this:

Time delayDefaultWhat it is for
Normal failure (1C)1 secondIgnores momentary utility blips before calling for a start
Transfer to emergency (2B)0Lets the generator stabilize before it picks up load
Retransfer to normal (3A)30 minutesConfirms utility is really back and stable before switching off the generator
Engine cooldown (2E)5 minutesUnloaded run time after retransfer, before the engine is told to stop

Look at that 30-minute retransfer delay. It means the generator is expected to carry the load for a good half hour after the lights come back on outside, plus five more minutes of cooldown. A facility manager who sees utility restored, decides "we made it," and stops watching the fuel gauge has miscounted by 35 minutes at the exact moment the tank is lowest.

What does refueling actually take on a live generator?

More coordination than a job-site fill. Four things decide whether this is a 30-minute stop or a three-hour problem, and all four are knowable in advance:

  • Where the tank is. A sub-base tank at grade is straightforward. A set on a roof, in a below-grade vault, or behind a locked gate with a fire lane in front of it is not.
  • Fill point and hose reach. Measure from where a truck can legally park to the fill. Do it on a sunny afternoon, not in the dark.
  • Day tank or main tank. NFPA 110 Section 7.9.2 requires fuel tanks be close enough for the engine's fuel pump lift to work, "or a fuel transfer pump and day tank shall be provided." If your set runs off a day tank, that transfer pump needs power — and if the main tank is dry, filling the day tank is the only thing that helps.
  • Who has keys and who signs. At 3 a.m. this is the most common delay on the list, and it costs nothing to fix now.

It is why we run 24/7 emergency delivery across the Denver metro rather than a phone tree. But a call the moment the alarm sounds still beats a call after the engine has taken air. One is a fuel delivery. The other is a fuel delivery plus a service call.

How do you keep it from happening at all?

Four things, in order of what they are worth:

  1. Know your run duration, not just your Class. NFPA 110 defines Class as the duration in hours the system must run at full rated output without refueling. Class 2 means two hours. A building can be fully code-compliant and be two hours from needing a fuel truck. Compliant is not the same as covered.
  2. Put a fuel call in the outage procedure, at the top. Not "if the outage goes long" — the first hour, every time. Fuel ordered and not needed costs you a topped-off tank. Fuel ordered late costs you a bled fuel system.
  3. Confirm the low-fuel alarm annunciates where a human actually is. A red light on a panel in a locked mechanical room at 2 a.m. is not an alarm.
  4. Order at a third of a tank. It leaves room for dispatch time, keeps the suction above the water and sediment on the bottom, and means nobody is bleeding a fuel system in the dark.

Frequently asked questions

Can you refuel a generator while it is running?

In most cases yes, and it is usually the better option than shutting the set down mid-outage. It requires a driver who has done it, a clear path to the fill point, and grounding and spill controls in place. Confirm the procedure with your generator service provider in advance, since some installations and some fire jurisdictions have their own restrictions.

How long does it take to restart a generator that ran out of fuel?

It depends on the engine and who is available. Beyond the fuel itself, the fuel system has to be bled and primed, and the filters often have to be changed because the engine pulled sediment from the bottom of the tank. On a modern set with a priming pump and a technician on site it can be quick. Without one, it is not.

Does a low fuel alarm mean the generator is about to shut down?

Not immediately, but it is the point to act. On the Cummins DQF-series panel, LOW FUEL trips at roughly 62 percent of tank level, and a separate CRITICAL LOW FUEL alarm indicates the level has reached tank bottom. The first alarm is your ordering window. The second is your last chance to shut down deliberately.

Will running out of fuel damage the generator?

It can. Cummins describes the purpose of its critical low fuel warning as giving the operator time to shut down before fuel runs out, preventing loss of prime or engine damage. At minimum you are looking at a bleed-and-prime and a filter change. The larger cost is usually the time the building spends without power while that happens.
