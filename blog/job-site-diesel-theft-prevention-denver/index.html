How Do You Stop Diesel Theft on a Denver Job Site?

Quick answer

You stop job-site diesel theft by removing the target and controlling the paperwork. Lock and fence the tank, meter every gallon in and out, reconcile fuel against machine hours weekly, and keep less fuel on site by scheduling deliveries closer to burn. Cameras help only if someone is assigned to review the footage.

By Erick Dykema Published Aug 7, 2026 Updated Aug 7, 2026 7 min read Reliability & Operations

The call always sounds the same. A superintendent walks the site at 6:30 on a Monday, the 500-gallon cube reads a third lower than Friday, and nothing explains it. No broken lock. No tire tracks. Just gallons that are gone.

Fuel theft is the loss almost nobody puts on a report. Too small to feel like a crime scene, too routine to escalate, so it gets absorbed into the fuel line item and forgotten. That is exactly why it repeats.

How common is job-site fuel theft in Denver, really?

Nobody has a clean number, and you should be suspicious of anyone who hands you one. When CBS Colorado reported on a run of Denver-metro fuel thefts in June 2026, the Denver Police Department said it had logged four fuel-theft cases in 2026 — while also saying it does not track stolen fuel as its own category. Englewood police documented two cases in early May. Officers told the station that victims often do not report at all, because there is rarely evidence to work with.

Treat those figures as a floor, not a measurement. Most of the eye-catching percentages circulating online trace back to security-vendor marketing rather than police or insurer data. You manage this from your own fuel log.

What is documented is the method. Thieves either siphoned the tanks or drilled straight through them, and CBS Colorado reported repairs of roughly $1,000 to $2,500 depending on damage. On a job site, a drilled tank is also a spill and a fire hazard sitting in your laydown yard.

What is the stolen diesel actually worth — legally?

Colorado grades theft by the value of what was taken, under C.R.S. 18-4-401(2). Here is the part most contractors do not know. Under C.R.S. 18-4-401(4)(a), when a person commits theft twice or more within a period of six months, two or more of those thefts may be aggregated and charged in a single count. Subsection (4)(b) allows the same for repeated thefts against one victim under a single scheme or course of conduct.

Read that with a fuel log in your hand. Forty gallons skimmed on a Thursday night is a petty offense and no detective is coming. The same forty gallons, documented eleven times across two months, is 440 gallons in one count — a felony. That is why the log outranks the camera.

What does Colorado expect a fuel tank to look like?

Colorado's storage tank rules live in 7 CCR 1101-14, administered by the Division of Oil and Public Safety. Read the scope first: the AST program covers tanks between 660 and 39,999 gallons, so most job-site cubes — 250, 500, 550 gallons — sit below it. The rules still matter. They are the clearest written statement of what this state considers adequate tank security, and they are what your fire inspector and your carrier will reach for:

  • Rule 3-2-1(g)(2) — tanks not enclosed in vaults are to be enclosed with a chain link fence at least 6 feet high, separated from the tank by at least 10 feet, with a gate secured against unauthorized entry.
  • Rule 3-2-1(g)(3) — tanks unsupervised for any period of time, or in isolated or remote areas, are to be secured and marked to identify the fire hazards. Where necessary to protect the tank from tampering or trespassing, the area is to be secured.
  • Rule 3-2-1(e) — tank outlets are to have a device, such as a normally closed solenoid valve, that prevents gravity flow from the tank to the dispenser.

"Unsupervised for any period of time" describes every job site in the metro between 5 p.m. and 6 a.m. Permit thresholds are separate and vary by fire district — those are covered in do you need a permit to store diesel on a Denver job site. Confirm both with your local authority having jurisdiction before you set a tank.

How do you tell theft from ordinary shrink?

Your fuel log lies to you for boring reasons before it lies to you for criminal ones: extended idling, cold starts, a machine that went to another job, an operator topping off a company truck without writing it down, a drifted meter. Rule those out first. Accusing a crew of theft when the answer was idle time is a mistake you only get to make once.

Timing separates the two. Ordinary variance spreads evenly across the week and scales with production. Theft clusters: Friday night to Monday morning, holidays, the week the site sat idle. It comes in round numbers, and it stops the moment the tank moves.

What should you do the morning you find fuel missing?

  1. Photograph the tank, the cap, the ground under it, and the meter reading before anyone touches anything.
  2. Check the shell and the ground for drilling or a leak, and treat pooled fuel as a spill.
  3. Write the loss into the fuel log: date, gallon figure, last known good reading. That entry is what a six-month aggregation is built from.
  4. File a police report even when the amount is small. It is the only way incident two ever connects to incident one.
  5. Ask your agent one question: is fuel covered under this builder's risk policy, and what is the deductible?
  6. Change one thing — move the tank into the light, cut the on-site volume, or switch to direct fueling. Repeating the same setup and hoping is not a control.

The clean version of all of this is to stop storing fuel you are not burning that week. Delivery straight to the equipment removes the tank, the fence, the permit conversation, and the theft target in one move — the economics are compared in wet hosing versus a job-site tank. On high burn with a long schedule a tank still wins. But if you are losing fuel and have not priced the alternative, you are paying for that tank twice.

Frequently asked questions

Is it worth filing a police report for a small fuel theft?

Yes. Colorado lets a prosecutor aggregate thefts: under C.R.S. 18-4-401(4)(a), two or more thefts committed within a period of six months may be charged in a single count. One report of 40 missing gallons goes nowhere. Eleven documented reports across two months is a felony-value case. The paperwork is the leverage.

Does a locking fuel cap actually stop diesel theft?

It stops the opportunist with a hose, which is most of them, and costs almost nothing. It does not stop a drill. CBS Colorado reported Denver and Englewood thieves drilling straight through tanks, with repairs running roughly $1,000 to $2,500. Pair the cap with a weekly reconciliation.

Can I put dyed off-road diesel in my crew's pickup trucks to save money?

No. Dyed diesel is legal in off-road equipment and illegal on public roads. The federal penalty under 26 U.S.C. 6715 is the greater of $1,000 or $10 for every gallon involved, and it increases with prior penalties. Ten gallons is a $1,000 exposure. Tell your crew that number.

Does Colorado require a job-site fuel tank to be fenced?

It depends on size. Colorado's AST rules in 7 CCR 1101-14 cover tanks from 660 to 39,999 gallons, and Rule 3-2-1(g)(2) calls for a chain link fence at least 6 feet high, 10 feet from the tank, with a secured gate. Most 250 to 550 gallon cubes fall below that scope. Confirm with your local fire authority.

What is the single most effective way to prevent job-site fuel theft?

Do not store fuel you are not burning that week. A tank on site is a standing target that has to be fenced, permitted, monitored, and reconciled. Direct-to-equipment delivery removes all of it. On a high-burn, long-duration job a tank still makes sense, but most sites carry more standing fuel than the schedule requires.
